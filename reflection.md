# Mess2Meal — Project Reflection

Developing Mess2Meal helped me understand how a practical technology solution can address a real coordination challenge. I began with a simple idea: make surplus food visible and help connect food providers with organisations or people who may need it. As I explored the problem, I realised that successful food redistribution involves much more than finding a nearby recipient. Availability, quantity, preparation time, storage information, recipient capacity and timely collection all affect whether a handover can take place.

This understanding shaped the workflow I developed. A donor can create a food listing, a recipient can register relevant preferences and capacity, and the system can generate an explainable shortlist of potentially suitable recipients. The listing then moves through separate stages—matching, acceptance, pickup and delivery—so that a suggested match is not mistaken for a completed donation. I kept the final handover as a recorded human-confirmed step.

I also considered the people involved in the process. My field visits covered college and hostel mess settings, a nearby restaurant/canteen, a bakery or food outlet, and an NGO stakeholder. The roles I focused on included mess workers, a restaurant owner, canteen workers and an NGO representative. These settings helped me frame the project around the practical coordination needs of food providers and recipient organisations. I have kept the documentation careful about distinguishing confirmed visit details from specific observations, measurements or quotations that require their own supporting notes.

Research into existing food-sharing initiatives, including the India Food Sharing Alliance and Robin Hood Army, also influenced how I positioned the project. I do not see Mess2Meal as a replacement for existing organisations or as an entirely new food donation network. I see it as a small coordination prototype that demonstrates how structured listings, recipient shortlisting and status tracking could support a local redistribution workflow.

From a technical perspective, I used Python, FastAPI, Streamlit, SQLite and Pydantic to build the prototype. I separated the dashboard, API and matching logic so that the main workflow could be understood and tested in smaller parts. The matching process uses eligibility checks and ranking factors such as recipient capacity, category preference, urgency and coordinator-entered distance. The reasons behind a suggestion are displayed to support review rather than making the decision opaque.

One important design decision was to keep the system's limits clear. The prototype does not verify recipients, calculate live GPS routes, send notifications or certify that food is safe. Food suitability and the actual handover require responsible human checks. The demonstration dataset is synthetic and is kept separate from real field evidence; it is useful for testing the application but cannot be used to claim real donations or impact.

AI tools supported my brainstorming, helped me compare possible approaches, organise the project structure and improve the documentation. However, I treated the suggestions as inputs to evaluate rather than decisions to accept automatically. This process reinforced the importance of checking assumptions, understanding the code and being transparent about the difference between a working prototype, sample data and evidence from real-world use.

If I continue developing Mess2Meal, I would first validate the core workflow with food providers and recipient organisations, then prioritise improvements based on their feedback. Features such as automated distance calculation, notifications and richer analytics can come later. My priority would be to make the essential process reliable, simple to use and clear about who is responsible at each stage.

Overall, this project strengthened my ability to move from an initial problem idea to research, system design, implementation and documentation. More importantly, it taught me that useful software is not defined only by the features it contains; it must fit the way people work and support decisions without claiming to replace human judgement.
